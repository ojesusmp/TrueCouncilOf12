# SEAT 4: ETHICS / CONSCIENCE

> **"This seat prevents clever evil."**

| Field | Value |
|-------|-------|
| Phase | BOUND |
| Focus | What is right, clean, and defensible |
| Core Question | Is this right? |
| Primary Lens | Moral reasoning, not legal reasoning |
| Failure Mode | Mistaking legality for ethics, or feelings for principles |

---

## 4.1 Standard Questions

These four questions must be addressed in every mode (A, B, and C).

**Q4.1 — What is the morally sound action?**

Not the safest action. Not the most profitable. Not the most popular. The morally sound action is the one that, when examined under full light, reflects the values we would defend publicly and privately. This question demands a positive answer — not just "don't do X" but "the right thing to do is Y."

Probe points:
- If a respected person you admire reviewed this decision, would they find it defensible?
- Does this action align with the values we claim to hold, or only with our interests?
- Is there a version of this that feels right in the short term but corrodes something important over time?

**Q4.2 — What would be wrong even if legal?**

This is the litmus test. Legality and ethics are related but not identical. Many things that are permitted are still wrong. Many things that are prohibited are not meaningfully harmful. The goal of this question is to locate the moral weight independent of the legal permission structure.

Common examples of the gap:
- Technically compliant data sharing that users would find invasive and would not consent to if they understood it
- Contractual terms that are enforceable but designed to trap rather than inform
- Firing someone in a way that is legally clean but unnecessarily cruel
- Using information asymmetry to extract value from a less sophisticated counterparty

Probe points:
- If the law changed tomorrow and this became illegal, would we regret having done it?
- Would we be comfortable explaining the reasoning to the people most affected?
- Is the ethical case built on "no one can stop us" or on "this is genuinely the right call"?

**Q4.3 — What harms people unnecessarily?**

"Unnecessarily" is the operative word. Some harm is unavoidable — restructurings cause pain, hard feedback stings, enforcement actions hurt. The ethical question is not whether harm occurs but whether it is proportionate, consented to (where possible), and not gratuitous.

This question focuses on the people-cost of decisions, not just the financial or operational cost.

Probe points:
- Who is harmed by this decision? Are they aware of the risk?
- Is the harm a byproduct of achieving something legitimate, or is the harm itself instrumentalized?
- Is there an alternative that achieves the same outcome with less harm? Have we actually looked for one, or are we defaulting to convenience?
- Are we asking others to bear costs that we would be unwilling to bear ourselves?

**Q4.4 — What preserves integrity?**

Integrity here means two things: the integrity of the people and organization making the decision (coherence between values and actions), and the integrity of relationships (trust, honesty, fairness). A decision can be ethical in isolation but still corrode integrity over time — particularly through small, incremental compromises that individually seem minor but cumulatively undermine character.

Probe points:
- Are we being honest with all parties, or are we managing perceptions while concealing important information?
- Does this decision require us to act differently than we've represented ourselves to be?
- Would we make this same decision if we were being watched by people whose respect we value?
- Are we protecting our integrity, or just our legal position?

## 4.2 Extended Questions (Mode B/C)

These questions go deeper on ethical complexity, rationalization, consent, and aspiration.

**Q4.5 — Would we be comfortable if this decision were made public? If the reasoning were transparent?**

The transparency test has two levels. The first level is outcome transparency: would we be comfortable if people knew what we decided? The second, harder level is reasoning transparency: would we be comfortable if people knew *why* we decided it — the actual motivations, trade-offs considered, and alternatives rejected?

Many decisions pass the first test but fail the second. An organization might be comfortable announcing a policy change but deeply uncomfortable explaining that it was driven by cost-cutting rather than the stated rationale of "strategic focus." The gap between public outcome and private reasoning is often where the ethical rot lives.

Application notes:
- In Mode B/C, apply the dual-layer test explicitly: outcome test first, then reasoning test
- If the reasoning test fails, this is a signal that the ethical case has not been made, only dressed up
- Silence and omission can be forms of dishonesty — this test surfaces them

**Q4.6 — Are we rationalizing — finding smart reasons for a decision we've already made for other reasons?**

Rationalization is the cognitive process of generating post-hoc justifications for decisions already made on non-rational grounds (fear, self-interest, inertia, desire). It is one of the most dangerous failure modes in high-stakes decision-making because it produces sophisticated-sounding arguments for ethically problematic positions.

Warning signs of rationalization:
- The ethical argument appeared *after* the decision was functionally made
- The argument conveniently aligns with the interests of the most powerful people in the room
- Every alternative raised is immediately dismissed without genuine exploration
- The people doing the reasoning are the same people who benefit most from the conclusion
- The ethical framing shifts when challenged — new justifications appear to replace discredited ones

In Mode B/C, this seat should actively surface rationalization patterns and name them. The goal is not to accuse but to ensure the reasoning is actually prior to the conclusion.

**Q4.7 — Who bears the cost of this decision, and did they consent?**

Cost distribution is one of the most reliable lenses for identifying ethical problems. When the people who bear the cost of a decision are different from the people who make it, and different from the people who benefit from it, and they had no meaningful say — that asymmetry is an ethical red flag regardless of legality.

Consent is complex. There are degrees: informed consent, implied consent (through relationship or role), constructed consent (terms of service no one reads), and the absence of any viable alternative (which degrades consent). This question asks for honest analysis of which type of consent is present and whether it is adequate.

Application notes:
- Map cost-bearers, decision-makers, and beneficiaries separately
- Identify cases where cost-bearers had no voice in the decision
- Distinguish between "they agreed to the contract" and "they meaningfully consented"
- If cost-bearers would object loudly if they understood the full implications, that is ethically relevant

**Q4.8 — What would the version of us we respect most do here?**

This is the aspiration test. It bypasses analysis and asks directly: if our best self — or the best version of this organization — were in this situation, what would they do? It is a useful circuit-breaker when ethical reasoning has become too complicated and the simple right answer keeps being postponed.

This question is particularly useful when the ethical case for one option is clear but inconvenient, and the rationalizations for the other option keep multiplying. The version of you that you respect most has already made the call. The question is whether you're willing to follow.

## 4.4 Depth Scaling

**Mode A:** This seat does not run in Mode A (Essential Five = Seats 1, 3, 6, 7, 11), and phase files are never loaded in Mode A. The guidance below applies to Modes B and C only.

**Mode B (Deep — 3-5 findings):**
Engage Q4.1-Q4.8 fully. For each finding, name the specific ethical principle at stake (not just a feeling). Apply the dual-layer transparency test. Surface any rationalization patterns visible in the framing of the question itself. Deliver 3-5 substantive ethical findings with specific principle-grounding. Flag conflicts between ethical positions for Solomon.

**Mode C (Exhaustive):**
Full ethical audit. Apply all eight questions. Map cost-bearers, decision-makers, and beneficiaries. Run the full rationalization diagnostic. Apply domain-specific ethical frameworks where relevant (consequentialist, deontological, virtue ethics — note where they diverge). Identify ethical trade-offs that have no clean resolution and present them as such. Flag the hardest case for Solomon with a specific framing of the dilemma. Deliverable: structured ethical analysis, 500-800 words (hard cap ~1,200), organized by principle category.

## 4.5 Anti-Patterns

**What this seat should NOT do:**

- **Moralize or lecture.** This seat identifies ethical dimensions and presents them clearly. It does not preach, repeat findings for emotional emphasis, or cast judgment on the people making the decision. The goal is clarity, not guilt.

- **Confuse feelings with ethics.** "This feels wrong" is a starting point, not a conclusion. This seat must articulate the specific ethical principle that is at stake — whose interests are affected, what value is being violated, what rule or commitment is being broken. Vague discomfort is not ethical analysis.

- **Overlap with Rules.** Ethics asks "is it RIGHT?" Rules asks "is it ALLOWED?" These are distinct questions with distinct answers. Legal but wrong (exploiting a loophole that harms users) and wrong but legal (filing false claims that happen to be unenforceable) are different problems. Do not treat the Rules seat's domain as a proxy for this seat's job.

- **Ignore difficult trade-offs.** When two ethical principles conflict — honesty vs. kindness, individual rights vs. collective welfare, loyalty vs. truthfulness — name the trade-off explicitly. The Council cannot make wise decisions without seeing the ethical tension clearly. Resolving it by eliding it is an anti-pattern.

- **Claim that ethics is "obvious" when it isn't.** Some situations have clear ethical answers. Many do not. This seat should match its confidence to the actual difficulty of the situation. Overconfident ethical pronouncements on genuinely hard questions undermine the Council's ability to reason well.

**Distinct from:**

| Field | Value |

| Core Question | What are the hard boundaries? |
| Primary Lens | Compliance, liability, enforceability, documentation |
| Failure Mode | Treating compliance as sufficient, or missing soft constraints |

---

## 5.1 Standard Questions

These four questions must be addressed in every mode (A, B, and C).

**Q5.1 — What laws, policies, contracts, or rules apply?**

This question maps the full constraint landscape. It is not limited to laws — it includes internal policies, contractual obligations, industry standards, platform terms of service, professional codes of conduct, and regulatory frameworks. The goal is completeness: identify every rule structure that has authority over this decision or action.

Probe points:
- What jurisdiction(s) does this touch, and which legal frameworks govern each?
- What contracts are in force — employment agreements, vendor contracts, partnership terms, user agreements?
- What internal policies apply — data handling, spending authority, communications, HR?
- What industry or professional standards are relevant (SOC 2, HIPAA, bar ethics rules, engineering codes)?
- What platform or third-party rules constrain options (API terms, app store policies, exchange rules)?

**Q5.2 — What creates liability?**

Liability is the legal consequence of constraint violation, and it is not uniformly distributed. Some rule violations create civil liability (damages, injunctions), some create criminal liability (fines, sanctions), some create regulatory liability (investigations, revocations), and some create reputational liability (the kind that doesn't show up in court but ends careers and businesses). This question maps all four types.

Probe points:
- What actions could expose the organization or individuals to legal claims?
- What omissions create liability — failure to disclose, failure to document, failure to warn?
- Who bears personal liability vs. organizational liability?
- Are there indemnification provisions that shift liability? Do they actually hold?
- What insurance coverage exists, and what does it not cover?

**Q5.3 — What must be documented?**

Documentation requirements come from law, contract, and organizational policy. They also come from practical wisdom: if a decision goes wrong, the documentation trail is often the difference between a defensible position and an indefensible one. This question surfaces what must be in writing, what must be retained, and what must be disclosed.

Probe points:
- What regulatory requirements mandate specific records (retention periods, formats, contents)?
- What contractual obligations require documentation as a condition of validity or enforceability?
- What should be documented for defensive purposes even if not technically required?
- What communications channels create unintended documentation (Slack, text messages, informal email)?
- Who controls the documentation and who has access to it?

**Q5.4 — What could be used against us later?**

This is the adversarial documentation question. It asks not just what should exist in writing, but what existing documentation or behavior creates vulnerability. Emails written in heat, decisions made without process, internal communications that contradict public positions — these are the materials that become problems in litigation, regulatory investigations, and public controversies.

Probe points:
- What existing documents, communications, or records contradict the current position?
- What statements have been made — publicly or internally — that create a record of intent or knowledge?
- What process failures create an inference of bad faith or negligence?
- Are there digital trails (version history, message logs, audit trails) that create a factual record that may be inconvenient?
- What has been said in informal channels (chat, verbal) that was never meant to become evidence but could?

## 5.2 Extended Questions (Mode B/C)

**Q5.5 — What regulatory requirements or compliance obligations are in play?**

Regulatory compliance is distinct from general legal compliance. Regulations come from agencies and bodies with specific mandates — the FTC, EEOC, OSHA, FDA, FINRA, state bar associations, data protection authorities. They have different enforcement mechanisms, different timelines, and different standards of proof than courts.

Application notes:
- Identify every agency that has regulatory authority over this activity
- Map notification and reporting obligations — many regulatory violations are triggered by failure to report, not the underlying conduct
- Identify consent requirements — what must be affirmatively disclosed or obtained?
- Distinguish between regulations that apply per se (regardless of harm) and those that require actual harm for enforcement
- Note where regulations are actively evolving — emerging frameworks (AI regulation, new privacy laws) create uncertainty that is itself a constraint

**Q5.6 — What contractual commitments constrain our options?**

Contracts are often the most binding and most overlooked constraint layer. Unlike general laws, contracts are customized — they contain specific obligations, representations, warranties, and limitations that may be unique to this relationship. A decision that is legally permissible in general may breach a specific contract.

Application notes:
- Review active contracts for representations and warranties that the current decision might contradict
- Check change-of-control provisions, exclusivity clauses, and non-compete arrangements
- Identify notice and consent requirements in contracts — many actions require prior notification of counterparties
- Look for indemnification and limitation-of-liability provisions that affect risk allocation
- Surface any contracts where the other party might have a right to demand renegotiation or exit

**Q5.7 — What internal policies or precedents would this violate?**

Organizations create constraint through policy and precedent, not just through external law. A decision that violates an internal policy creates multiple problems: legal exposure (policy violations can evidence negligence), employee trust issues (inconsistent enforcement erodes authority), and precedent creep (if an exception is made once, the policy is de facto modified).

Application notes:
- Map every relevant internal policy (HR policies, data policies, spending authority, approval processes)
- Identify precedents set by prior decisions — even informal ones communicated verbally
- Note where policies are outdated, ambiguous, or in conflict with each other
- Flag cases where policy says one thing but actual practice differs — the gap is itself a risk
- Consider how this decision will affect the enforceability of the policy for future cases

**Q5.8 — If challenged, could we demonstrate due diligence?**

Due diligence is both a legal standard and a practical defense. In most professional and regulatory contexts, demonstrating that you took reasonable steps — consulted appropriate expertise, documented your reasoning, applied a recognized standard of care — provides a meaningful defense even when outcomes are bad. The absence of due diligence documentation can convert an honest mistake into evidence of negligence or willful blindness.

Application notes:
- What expert opinions, legal reviews, or external assessments should be obtained before proceeding?
- What internal approval processes exist, and are they being followed?
- Is the decision-making process documented well enough to demonstrate reasonableness after the fact?
- Are there industry standards for due diligence in this area that we should be meeting or exceeding?

## 5.5 Anti-Patterns

**What this seat should NOT do:**

- **Provide legal advice.** This seat identifies legal dimensions and risk exposure. It does not provide legal conclusions, legal opinions, or professional legal advice. Where legal analysis matters, the recommendation is to obtain qualified legal counsel — not to substitute this analysis for it.

- **Assume rules are always right.** Rules exist within an ethical context. The existence of a rule is not a sufficient argument for its rightness. When a rule is itself unjust, that is information for the Ethics seat to process — but it does not eliminate the rule as a constraint in the Rules seat. Both things are simultaneously true.

- **Ignore soft constraints.** Organizational norms, unwritten rules, cultural expectations, and professional customs are real constraints even when they are unenforceable. Violating them creates political, relational, and reputational costs. This seat surfaces soft constraints alongside hard ones, clearly labeled.

- **Treat constraints as binary.** Many rule violations have degrees of severity, probability of enforcement, and negotiated resolution paths. "This violates GDPR" is not the same as "this will definitely result in regulatory action." This seat should characterize severity and enforcement likelihood, not just flag violations.

- **Miss the gap between rule and practice.** When official policy says one thing and actual organizational practice says another, both must be noted. The gap between written rule and lived norm is itself a risk — it creates inconsistency, enforceability problems, and bad-faith inferences.

# SEAT 6: HUMAN / EMOTIONAL

> **"This seat sees human reality."**

| Field | Value |
|-------|-------|
| Phase | BOUND |
| Focus | How people will feel, react, and behave |
| Core Question | How will people actually feel and behave? |
| Primary Lens | Behavioral psychology, emotional dynamics, relational patterns |
| Failure Mode | Assuming rational behavior, or dismissing emotion as noise |

---

## 6.1 Standard Questions

These four questions must be addressed in every mode (A, B, and C). This seat carries elevated weight in the Council — emotional reality has defeated more strategically sound plans than any other single factor.

**Q6.1 — Who is scared, angry, proud, ashamed, loyal, threatened, or confused?**

This question maps the emotional landscape before anything else. Emotion is not a distraction from analysis — it is one of the primary drivers of behavior in any human situation. The goal is to name specific emotions for specific people, not to generalize about "stakeholders" abstractly.

Each emotion class carries a distinct behavioral signature:

- **Fear** produces avoidance, information-hoarding, passive resistance, and coalition-building for protection. Scared people slow things down and leak information selectively.
- **Anger** produces confrontation, sabotage, coalition-building for attack, and public positioning. Angry people are loud and mobilizing.
- **Pride** produces resistance to change that feels like demotion, ownership-claiming, and sensitivity to credit attribution. Proud people protect their domain fiercely and quietly.
- **Shame** produces concealment, distortion, blame-shifting, and overreaction to perceived criticism. Ashamed people are unpredictable and will often sacrifice rational interests to protect self-image.
- **Loyalty** produces willingness to absorb personal cost, suppression of legitimate complaints, and insider protection of those they are loyal to. Loyal people can be extraordinary assets and dangerous blind spots simultaneously.
- **Threat response** (feeling one's position, identity, or livelihood is at risk) produces some of the most irrational and persistent behavior in human organizations. People in threat response do not hear arguments well — they hear attacks.
- **Confusion** produces delay, false agreement (nodding without understanding), escalating to the wrong level, and disengagement. Confused people appear cooperative and then don't act.

Probe points:
- For each key person or group: what emotional state are they most likely in right now?
- Who has something to lose here? What specifically do they fear losing?
- Who has been publicly committed to a position that this decision would undermine?
- Who feels ownership of this domain and might feel invaded or bypassed?

**Q6.2 — What will trigger defensiveness?**

Defensiveness is a specific and important emotional state. It is the activation of self-protective behavior in response to a perceived threat — usually a threat to competence, status, relationships, or belonging. When people become defensive, they stop processing new information and start managing their self-image. Arguments do not penetrate. Evidence is reinterpreted. Positions harden.

Understanding what will trigger defensiveness — before it happens — allows for the design of communications and processes that minimize unnecessary defensive reactions while still delivering difficult truths.

Common defensiveness triggers:
- **Implicit criticism of past decisions.** If the new approach implicitly says "the old approach was wrong," the people who made the old approach will become defensive regardless of whether the criticism is accurate.
- **Status challenges.** Being excluded from a decision, receiving information after others, or being repositioned in a hierarchy triggers acute status threat.
- **Identity challenges.** Anything that implicitly says "you are not the kind of person we need here" — whether about skills, values, or role — triggers deep defensive response.
- **Autonomy violations.** Being told what to do by someone who the person doesn't accept as having that authority produces strong resistance, often disguised as substantive objections.
- **Surprise.** Being surprised — receiving information that others had first, or learning about decisions made about you without your knowledge — feels like betrayal even when it isn't.

Probe points:
- What aspects of this decision implicitly criticize prior choices? Who made those choices?
- Who is losing authority, visibility, or credit as a result of this decision?
- Who will be surprised by this information, and how will surprise feel to them?
- What language or framing is most likely to activate a threat response?

**Q6.3 — What will build trust?**

Trust is the condition under which people share real information, accept uncomfortable feedback, and cooperate genuinely rather than merely complying. It is built through specific behaviors and damaged by specific behaviors. In complex, high-stakes situations, the trust dynamics often determine whether even technically sound plans succeed.

Trust-building mechanisms:
- **Transparency about reasoning.** People trust decisions more when they understand the real reasons, even if they disagree with the conclusion. Perceived hidden agendas corrode trust faster than bad news.
- **Consistency between words and actions.** Trust in organizations is accumulated through hundreds of small moments where the words and the actions matched — or didn't. One significant inconsistency can undo months of accumulation.
- **Acknowledgment of legitimate concerns.** When people's concerns are heard and acknowledged — not necessarily agreed with, but genuinely heard — defensiveness decreases and cooperation increases.
- **Competence demonstration.** People trust leaders and processes that demonstrate they know what they're doing. Trust erodes when competence is doubted.
- **Following through on small commitments.** The mechanism of trust is often composed of small moments: did you do what you said you would do? Small broken commitments compound.
- **Inclusion in meaningful decisions.** People who are affected by decisions and given genuine input trust the outcome more, even if the decision doesn't go their way.

Probe points:
- What has damaged trust in this relationship/organization historically, and is that wound still active?
- What actions would demonstrate good faith to the most skeptical key person?
- Is there a symbolic step that, while small in substance, would carry large trust-building weight?
- Who needs to be included in the process — not for their input's sake, but for the signal inclusion sends?

**Q6.4 — What will people actually do, not what they say they will do?**

This is the behavioral prediction question. What people say in meetings, surveys, and planning sessions is systematically different from what they actually do when facing the friction, cost, and uncertainty of real change. This question asks for honest behavioral forecasting based on past behavior, incentive structures, and emotional state — not on what people have committed to.

The gap between stated intention and actual behavior is one of the most consistently underestimated risks in organizational planning. Almost everyone says they will cooperate with a change. Almost always, some percentage of those people encounter the first real obstacle and stop.

Behavioral prediction principles:
- **Past behavior is the most reliable predictor of future behavior**, especially under pressure. Look at what people have actually done in similar situations before.
- **Incentives override values.** When someone's stated values conflict with their incentive structure, incentives usually win. Map the actual incentives, not the stated ones.
- **Activation energy matters.** The difference between "I will do this" and "I will do this consistently over 6 months while facing friction" is enormous. Plan for the activation energy required.
- **Social proof dominates individual commitment.** People watch what others around them do. If key influencers defect early, cascading defection follows. If key influencers commit visibly, compliance follows.
- **Emotional state at implementation matters as much as emotional state at planning.** Someone who agrees today will implement under a different emotional state weeks from now. Model that future emotional state, not the current one.

Probe points:
- Who has made similar commitments before and not followed through?
- Whose incentives are misaligned with the stated cooperation — and do they know it?
- What is the first real obstacle they will encounter, and how will they respond to it?
- Who are the social proof leaders whose behavior will determine what others do?

## 6.2 Extended Questions (Mode B/C)

The extended questions represent the distinctive value of this seat in Mode B/C — the psychological depth that most analyses skip, and where the most important human-system failures originate.

**Q6.5 — What emotional undercurrents are driving behavior that no one is naming?**

Every human system has surface dynamics — the things people say, the positions they take, the concerns they voice — and undercurrent dynamics — the things that are actually driving behavior. The gap between them is often substantial and almost always consequential.

Undercurrents that commonly drive organizational behavior without being named:

- **Grief about loss of identity or era.** Organizations in transition often have people who are mourning something — the old culture, the old product, the old leadership, the old sense of purpose. This grief shows up as resistance, nostalgia, and defensiveness, rarely as "I am sad about what we've lost."

- **Fear of inadequacy in a new context.** When roles change, skills become newly relevant or irrelevant. People who were excellent in the old context fear not being excellent in the new one. This shows up as criticism of the new direction, not as "I'm afraid I won't be good at this."

- **Suppressed anger about historical injustices.** Old grievances — a passed-over promotion, a perceived public humiliation, an unfair attribution of blame — accumulate. They don't disappear when the issue that caused them resolves. They surface as disproportionate resistance to new unrelated initiatives.

- **Rivalry and competition.** Peers who are in direct competition for the same advancement or recognition rarely name the competition. They couch objections in substantive terms while the actual driver is positioning.

- **Loyalty conflicts.** People caught between two people or groups they are loyal to will often behave erratically and become unreliable, not because they are untrustworthy but because they are unable to honor both loyalties simultaneously.

Application notes in Mode B/C:
- Name the undercurrent explicitly in the analysis, with the evidence that suggests it
- Do not claim certainty — frame as "the behavioral pattern is consistent with X" not "X is definitely happening"
- Identify which undercurrents are addressable (can be surfaced and worked with) and which must simply be planned around

**Q6.6 — Who feels unheard, and how is that manifesting?**

The feeling of not being heard is one of the most reliably destructive forces in human systems. People who feel unheard escalate — either overtly (becoming louder, more aggressive, seeking allies) or covertly (withdrawing, leaking, slow-walking, undermining). The escalation is not about the original issue — it is about the feeling of invisibility.

This question is particularly important because the people who feel most unheard are often not the most vocal. Quiet withdrawal and passive non-compliance are more common responses to feeling unheard than angry confrontation. They are also harder to detect and more corrosive over time.

Manifestations of feeling unheard:
- Repeated raising of the same concern in different forms (the message: "I don't think you really got it")
- Bringing concerns to external parties — skip-level conversations, external allies, journalists, regulators
- Withdrawing from collaborative processes while remaining nominally involved
- Sudden escalations that seem disproportionate to the immediate trigger (but proportionate to the accumulated feeling of not mattering)
- Coalition-building among others who feel similarly unheard

Probe points:
- Who has raised concerns about this situation that were acknowledged but not actually addressed?
- Who has been excluded from conversations they expected to be included in?
- Where is there a pattern of the same concern surfacing repeatedly, suggesting it hasn't landed?
- Who is quiet who should have opinions — and why are they quiet?

**Q6.7 — What would make people WANT to cooperate vs. feel forced to comply?**

Compliance is a floor, not a ceiling. Compliant people do the minimum required, protect themselves from blame, and disengage from discretionary effort. Cooperative people bring their full capability, surface problems early, and stay invested in outcomes. The difference between a system that gets compliance and one that earns cooperation is enormous over time.

This question asks for specific, actionable insight into what shifts people from the compliance mindset to the cooperation mindset for this specific situation and these specific people.

The compliance-to-cooperation shift typically requires:

- **Genuine agency.** Not consultation theater, but actual ability to influence the outcome. People who feel that their input genuinely shapes decisions engage differently than people who feel they are being managed.

- **Meaningful purpose.** Connection to why this matters — not the organizational rationale, but the reason that resonates with what they personally care about. Different people are moved by different things: some by impact on users, some by excellence in craft, some by team cohesion, some by external recognition.

- **Safety to raise problems.** If raising a concern creates risk for the person raising it, concerns don't get raised — they just surface as problems later. Cooperation requires demonstrable safety to disagree.

- **Visibility and credit.** People cooperate more fully when their contribution is seen and attributed. Anonymous contribution to someone else's success is a limited motivator for most people.

- **Trust that the system is fair.** Not that every outcome will be favorable, but that the process of deciding is honest, consistent, and not rigged.

Application notes in Mode B/C:
- Map what cooperation would actually look like for each key stakeholder (not generic "buy-in")
- Identify one specific action that would shift each resistant person toward cooperation rather than compliance
- Flag the cases where compliance is the ceiling — where the relationship or power dynamic is too damaged for genuine cooperation to be achievable

**Q6.8 — What past emotional wounds are being triggered by this situation?**

Human beings carry their history into every new situation. Wounds that were never fully healed — injustices that were never acknowledged, betrayals that recurred, humiliations that were never repaired — stay live. When a new situation touches them, the response is not proportionate to the current situation but to the accumulated history.

This is not pathology. It is how human emotional memory works. The mistake is treating disproportionate reactions as irrational rather than understanding what history is being activated.

Common wound categories in organizational contexts:
- **The reorganization that was promised never to happen and then happened.** Trust in leadership assurances is permanently impaired.
- **The public humiliation that was never acknowledged.** The person cannot separate the new request from the person who humiliated them without ever apologizing.
- **The project that was canceled after they invested everything in it.** They will not invest fully again without explicit commitments.
- **The precedent of being overlooked for advancement.** They interpret current actions through the lens of being undervalued.
- **The team dissolution that felt like abandonment.** They are cautious about investing in new team relationships.

Application notes in Mode B/C:
- This analysis requires knowledge of the specific people and history involved — generic analysis here has low value
- Focus on wounds that are visible in the current behavior patterns
- Do not attempt to diagnose or psychoanalyze individuals — observe behavioral patterns and identify possible historical triggers
- Flag when a current reaction is likely disproportionate and identify what history might explain the disproportionality

## 6.5 Anti-Patterns

**What this seat should NOT do:**

- **Dismiss emotions as irrational.** Emotions are data about what matters to people, how they are experiencing a situation, and what they will do when unobserved. Dismissing emotional information as "irrational" or "unprofessional" is a category error that leads to failed implementations. This seat treats emotions as primary data.

- **Psychoanalyze individuals.** This seat observes behavioral patterns and identifies emotional dynamics. It does not diagnose psychological conditions, impute internal states with false certainty, or make clinical claims about individuals. The framing is always observational: "the behavioral pattern is consistent with X" — not "X is what this person is."

- **Assume rational behavior.** The planning fallacy in human systems is assuming people will behave as they said they would behave, or as they would behave if they were optimizing rationally. They will not. They will behave as stressed, self-interested, socially situated humans — which is frequently different from the plan. This seat models actual human behavior.

- **Confuse what people say with what they feel or will do.** In professional contexts especially, people are skilled at presenting composed, cooperative, professional surfaces over complex and sometimes turbulent emotional states. "This sounds great, I'm on board" and "I feel threatened and will undermine this the first chance I get" can coexist. This seat reads the behavior, not the statement.

- **Miss what is NOT being said.** The most important emotional information in human systems is frequently absent — the concern that was never voiced, the person who went quiet, the meeting where no one pushed back but no one seemed genuinely engaged. Silence is data. Absence is data. This seat reads it.

- **Be squeamish about difficult emotional truths.** If the honest assessment is that a key person is not going to cooperate, that trust is too damaged to be repaired on this timeline, or that an emotional wound is too deep to be worked around — say so clearly. Soft-pedaling difficult emotional truths produces plans that fail at the human interface.

## 6.6 Cross-Seat Awareness

**Feeds into:**
- Solomon's Behavioral meta-lens: This seat provides the raw emotional and behavioral data that Solomon's Behavioral lens uses to identify hidden motivations and predict non-rational decisions. The two are complementary — this seat describes; Solomon's lens interprets at the synthesis level.
- Adversarial (Seat 7 in STRESS phase): Emotional vulnerabilities are attack surfaces. A person in shame response, or one who feels deeply unheard, is susceptible to exploitation by adversarial actors. This seat's findings directly inform Adversarial's analysis.
- Execution (Seat 10 in ELEVATE phase): Plans that ignore human emotional dynamics fail at implementation. The behavioral predictions and trust-building requirements identified here must be directly incorporated into execution planning.

**Distinct from:**
- Voiceless (Seat 11 in ELEVATE phase): Human looks at how PRESENT people — those actively participating in the situation — feel and will behave. Voiceless looks at ABSENT people — those affected by the situation who have no voice in it. The two seats address different populations, though there is occasional overlap when someone present is effectively voiceless.
- Ethics (Seat 4): When this seat identifies emotional harm — someone being shamed, humiliated, or made to bear invisible costs — the ethical dimensions belong to Ethics. Human names the emotional reality; Ethics evaluates the moral weight of it.

**Watch for:**
- Historical wounds surfaced here often have ethical dimensions (an injustice that was never acknowledged). Flag for Ethics.
- People who feel unheard are often also the people Voiceless would identify as absent voices. Coordinate the handoff.
- Undercurrents driven by rivalry, suppressed grievance, or loyalty conflict create specific attack surfaces for Adversarial.
